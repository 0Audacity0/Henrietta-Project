The Astronomer

Henrietta Swan Leavitt

Born on July 4, 1868, in Lancaster, Massachusetts, Henrietta Swan Leavitt grew up in a world where women were largely excluded from scientific careers. She studied at Oberlin College and later at Radcliffe College (the women's affiliate of Harvard University), where she discovered a passion for astronomy.

After graduation, she volunteered at the Harvard College Observatory, and was eventually hired by the legendary astronomer Edward Charles Pickering as one of his "Human Computers" — a team of women tasked with analyzing photographic plates of stars.

It takes a remarkable person to reshape our understanding of the universe while being barred from even pointing a telescope.

Leavitt suffered from severe hearing loss that worsened throughout her life. Despite this, she worked tirelessly — measuring the brightness of thousands of stars from photographic glass plates. In doing so, she discovered something that would fundamentally change astronomy forever.

The Leavitt Law (Period-Luminosity Relationship): Leavitt discovered that a class of pulsating stars called Cepheid variables had a precise, measurable relationship between how long they took to brighten and dim (their period) and how intrinsically bright they were (their luminosity). If you know a star's true brightness and can measure how bright it appears, you can calculate exactly how far away it is.

This gave astronomers a "standard candle" — a reliable yardstick to measure the universe. Edwin Hubble used Leavitt's law to prove that the Andromeda "nebula" was a separate galaxy millions of light-years away, fundamentally expanding humanity's conception of the cosmos. Her discovery underpins all modern cosmological distance measurements, including the calculation of the universe's age and rate of expansion.

The Basics

What Is a Star?

A star is an enormous, luminous ball of plasma held together by its own gravity. At its core, temperatures and pressures are so extreme that hydrogen atoms are crushed together in a process called nuclear fusion — the same reaction that powers a hydrogen bomb, but on a continuous, self-sustaining scale lasting billions of years.

This fusion converts hydrogen into helium, releasing tremendous amounts of energy as light and heat. That outward radiation pressure perfectly counterbalances the inward pull of gravity — a delicate equilibrium called hydrostatic equilibrium — keeping the star from collapsing under its own weight or flying apart.

Stars are born inside vast clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. When a region of a nebula becomes dense enough, gravity takes over, causing it to collapse inward. As it compresses, it heats up. When the core temperature reaches roughly 10 million Kelvin, nuclear fusion ignites, and a star is born.

Stars live for millions to billions of years, and when they die, they return their material to space — seeding future generations of stars, planets, and eventually, life. As Carl Sagan famously said: we are made of star stuff.

Chemistry

What Are Stars Made Of?

Stars are composed almost entirely of the two lightest elements in the universe — elements forged in the first moments after the Big Bang. The heavier elements were made later, inside stars themselves, and scattered through space when stars exploded.

Those "other" elements — iron, neon, nitrogen, silicon, and more — were forged in the nuclear furnaces of earlier stars over billions of years. Every iron atom in your blood, every calcium atom in your bones, every carbon atom in your DNA was created inside a dying star. The atoms that make up everything you see around you were once the core of an ancient sun.

On a perfectly clear, dark night — away from city light pollution — a human eye can see roughly 2,500 stars at any given moment from one hemisphere. Across the entire sky (both hemispheres combined), about 5,000 stars are bright enough to be seen without a telescope.

That sounds like a lot, but it's a breathtaking undercount. The Milky Way alone contains an estimated 200–400 billion stars. And there are roughly 2 trillion galaxies in the observable universe. The total estimated star count exceeds 10²⁴ — that's more stars than grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches combined.

Our Nearest Neighbor

The Closest Star to Earth

The closest star to Earth is, of course, our own Sun — a perfectly average yellow dwarf star about 93 million miles (150 million km) away. Light from the Sun reaches us in just about 8 minutes and 20 seconds.

The closest star outside our solar system is Proxima Centauri, a small red dwarf star that is part of the Alpha Centauri triple-star system. It sits approximately 4.24 light-years away — roughly 25 trillion miles. Light from Proxima takes over four years to reach us. If we could travel at the speed of our fastest spacecraft, it would take over 70,000 years to get there.

Intriguingly, Proxima Centauri hosts at least two known exoplanets — Proxima b sits within the star's habitable zone, where liquid water could potentially exist. Whether life could survive there under the frequent stellar flares from this active red dwarf is one of astronomy's most exciting open questions.

Is the Sun the largest star? Far from it. The Sun is remarkably average by cosmic standards. The largest known star is Stephenson 2-18 (also called St2-18), a red hypergiant roughly 2,150 times the radius of the Sun. If placed where the Sun is, its surface would extend past the orbit of Saturn. The Sun contains 99.8% of the mass of the entire solar system — yet it is a tiny speck compared to the true giants of the universe.

An Optical Phenomenon

Why Do Stars Twinkle?

Stars twinkle — astronomers call it stellar scintillation — because of Earth's atmosphere. Starlight must travel through many layers of air with different temperatures, densities, and humidity levels. Each of these layers bends (refracts) light slightly differently, and since these atmospheric pockets are constantly moving and mixing, the path the light takes shifts rapidly and randomly.

To our eyes, this looks like the star is flickering, shimmering, or changing color. Stars near the horizon twinkle more intensely because their light travels through a thicker slice of atmosphere to reach us.

Planets don't twinkle (much). Planets are close enough to appear as tiny discs rather than a single point — the atmospheric distortions across their visible disc average out, resulting in a steadier glow. This is a useful trick: a bright object that shimmers is a star; one that shines steadily is likely a planet.

From space — outside Earth's atmosphere — stars don't twinkle at all. The Hubble Space Telescope takes such sharp images precisely because it operates above the distorting atmosphere.

Patterns in the Sky

Constellations

A constellation is a recognized pattern of stars in the night sky, named after mythological figures, animals, or objects. Officially, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) recognizes 88 constellations, which together divide the entire celestial sphere into mapped regions — like countries on a map of the sky.

Most Western constellation names and stories come from ancient Greek and Roman mythology, though Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese, Indigenous, and other cultures all developed their own separate star patterns across history. The oldest known star catalog, the MUL.APIN, was recorded in Babylon around 1200 BCE.

Why do constellations only come out at night? They don't — the stars are always there. During the day, sunlight scatters so brilliantly through Earth's atmosphere that it overwhelms the faint light of distant stars. The sky fills with blue scattered light, completely drowning out the dim stars. At night, with the Sun below the horizon, that overwhelming light source is gone, and our eyes can finally detect the faint photons that have traveled light-years to reach us.

During a total solar eclipse, when the Moon briefly blocks the Sun's disc, stars and planets do become briefly visible in the middle of the day — a breathtaking reminder that they never actually go anywhere.

Common Questions

Brightness, Distance & More

Why are some stars bright and others dim?

A star's apparent brightness depends on two factors: how intrinsically luminous it is (its true energy output) and how far away it is. Brightness falls off with the square of distance — a star twice as far looks four times dimmer.

The brightest star in the night sky, Sirius, is dazzling partly because it truly is very luminous (about 25 times more luminous than the Sun) and partly because it is close — just 8.6 light-years away. Meanwhile, many red giant stars are intrinsically millions of times more luminous than Sirius but appear dim because they are thousands of light-years away.

Astronomers use two measurements: apparent magnitude (how bright a star looks from Earth) and absolute magnitude (how bright it would appear if placed exactly 32.6 light-years away). The difference reveals the star's distance. This is exactly the principle Henrietta Leavitt's Period-Luminosity Law leveraged — turning variable stars into cosmic distance markers.

How far away are the stars?

Stellar distances are so vast that astronomers use the light-year — the distance light travels in one year, about 5.88 trillion miles (9.46 trillion km). The nearby stars in our galactic neighborhood are a few to a few dozen light-years away. The most distant stars in the Milky Way are tens of thousands of light-years away. The Andromeda Galaxy — the farthest object visible to the naked eye — is about 2.537 million light-years away.

The most distant individual star ever observed, named Earendel, was detected by the James Webb Space Telescope at a staggering distance of approximately 12.9 billion light-years — meaning we're seeing it as it was less than a billion years after the Big Bang.

How long do stars live?

A star's lifespan is determined by its mass — and counterintuitively, more massive stars live shorter lives. They burn through their fuel at a furious rate. A star 20 times the Sun's mass may live only a few million years before exploding as a supernova. Our Sun is middle-aged at about 4.6 billion years old, with roughly 5 billion years left. The smallest red dwarf stars — a fraction of the Sun's mass — are so frugal with their fuel that they could theoretically burn for trillions of years, far longer than the current age of the universe.

What happens when a star dies?

A star's death depends on its mass. Stars like our Sun will eventually swell into a red giant, consuming the inner planets, then shed their outer layers as a glowing planetary nebula, leaving behind a dense, Earth-sized core called a white dwarf. More massive stars end in a spectacular supernova explosion, briefly outshining entire galaxies. The core collapses into either a neutron star (an object the size of a city with the mass of the Sun) or, if massive enough, a black hole — where gravity becomes so intense that not even light can escape.

What color are stars?

Stars come in a range of colors that directly reflect their surface temperature. Cooler stars (around 3,000–4,000 K) appear red or orange — like Betelgeuse or Antares. Medium-temperature stars like our Sun appear yellow-white. Very hot stars (above 10,000 K) shine brilliant blue-white — like Rigel or Sirius. The color sequence, from coolest to hottest, is: red → orange → yellow → white → blue-white. Astronomers classify stars using the OBAFGKM spectral type system (remembered with the mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy Kiss Me").

Do stars move?

Yes — all stars are in motion, orbiting the center of their galaxy. Our Sun, along with everything in the solar system, orbits the Milky Way's center at roughly 514,000 mph (828,000 km/h), taking about 225–250 million years to complete one orbit (called a cosmic year). Individual stars also have their own proper motions. However, these movements are so small relative to their enormous distances that the constellations look virtually identical to how they appeared to ancient astronomers thousands of years ago. Over hundreds of thousands of years, though, the familiar constellation shapes do gradually shift and distort.
