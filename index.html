Who was Henrietta Swan Leavitt?

She was an American astronomer who did some of the most important work in 20th century astronomy. Women were not allowed to run telescopes at Harvard back then, but they could study the photographic plates.

  1. 1868She was born on July 4 in Lancaster, Massachusetts, as the daughter of a Congregational minister.
  2. 1892She Graduated from the society for Collegiate Instruction of Women, which became Radcliffe College.
  3. 1893She started at Harvard College Observatory as a volunteer. Around 1895 she is paid 30 cents an hour. And an illness left her with progressive hearing loss.
  4. 1902She joined the staff under director Edward Pickering and leads the photographic brightness measurements. She sets up a standard scale of star magnitudes still used as a reference.
  5. 1908Publishes 1,777 variable stars found in the Magellanic Clouds. Over her career she finds about 2,400, more than half of all known at the time.
  6. 1912She shows that in the Small Magellanic Cloud, brighter variable stars take longer to "pulse". This is the period luminosity relation.
  7. 1913–1924Others calibrate her rule with nearby stars. Edwin Hubble then uses it to prove the Andromeda “nebula” is a separate galaxy far beyond the Milky Way.
  8. 1921She TRAGICALLY dies of cancer on December 12 in Cambridge, Massachusetts, at the age of 53. A Swedish mathematician tried to nominate her for the Nobel Prize in 1925, not knowing she had died.

Why her discovery mattered

All the stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud are about the same distance from us, so differences in how bright they looked meant real differences in power. Leavitt saw that for Cepheid stars, the longer the cycle, the more luminous the star. Once one Cepheid’s distance is known, every Cepheid becomes a measuring tape. Before this, nobody could reliably say how far away the faint patches in the sky were. After it, the universe had a scale, and later the proof that it was expanding.

Twelve questions about stars

What is a star?

A star is a huge ball of hot, glowing gas (plasma) held together by its own gravity. In the core, hydrogen atoms fuse into helium, releasing the energy that makes the star shine. Our Sun is ACTUALLY a star, how shocking!!!!

What are stars made of?

Mostly hydrogen, about 73% by mass, and helium, about 25%. The remaining couple of percent is heavier elements such as oxygen, carbon and iron, which earlier generations of stars made and scattered when they died.

How many stars can you see at night?

Under a dark, clear sky with no moon, you can see roughly 2,000–2,500 stars at once. Over the whole year and both hemispheres, about 9,000 stars are visible to the naked eye. City lights cut this to a few dozen.

How many stars are there?

The Milky Way holds an estimated 100 to 400 billion stars. With perhaps a trillion or more galaxies in the observable universe, astronomers estimate somewhere around 10²² to 10²⁴ stars. Nobody has counted them, so these are estimates.

What is the closest star to Earth?

The closest star is the Sun, at about 93.2 million miles. Light takes 8⅓ minutes to reach us. The nearest star beyond the Sun is Proxima Centauri, 4.24 light years away, about 24.85 trillion miles. It is a small red dwarf, making it too dim to see without a telescope.

Is the Sun the largest star?

Short answer: no. The Sun, as mentioned before, is an average sized star. Red supergiants like UY Scuti are more than a thousand times wider, and if one sat where the Sun is, it would swallow the orbits of the inner planets and maybe outer planets. Exact sizes of these giants are debated. Many stars are smaller than the Sun aswell.

Why do stars do the twinkling thing?

Starlight travels for years in a straight line, then meets Earth’s moving, uneven air. Pockets of warm and cool air bend it back and forth, so the star seems to flicker and shift color. Stars are so far away that they look like single points, which makes the effect strong. Planets look somewhat like tiny discs and twinkle far less. This is called scintillation. From space, stars don’t do the twinkling thing.

What are constellations?

Constellations are patterns people have traced between stars, like those connect the dots pictures. The International Astronomical Union has 88 constellations that divide up the whole sky. The stars in a pattern are usually at very different distances and aren’t related.

Why do stars only come out at night?

They don’t. Stars are up all day, but the Sun lights up our atmosphere and scatters blue light everywhere, drowning out their sad, little glow. At night our side of Earth faces away from the Sun, the sky goes dark, and the stars show. Astronauts can see them in daytime, since there’s no air to scatter the light.

Why are some stars bright and others dim?

Three things: how much light the star truly puts out, how far away it is, and how much dust sits in between. Sirius is the brightest star at night partly because it’s close, only 8.6 light years away, and it’s about 25 times more luminous than the Sun. A far off star can be thousands of times more powerful and still look faint. Astronomers separate apparent brightness from true brightness, and Leavitt’s work let them do so for distant stars.

How far away are the stars?

Very far. One light year, the distance light covers in a year, is about 5.88 trillion miles. Nearby stars are 4 to a few dozen light years away. Most stars you can see are within a few thousand. The Milky Way is about 100,000 light years across, and the Andromeda galaxy is around 2.5 million light years away.

How do we measure those distances?

For close stars, astronomers watch how a star seems to shift as Earth orbits the Sun, called parallax. Farther out, they use Cepheids and Leavitt’s rule. Beyond that, other markers like exploding stars take over.
